# GHK-Cu history (journal draft)

Status: draft

A journal article for `/journal/ghk-cu-history`. It explains how Copper Tripeptide-1 went from a plasma fraction to a routine serum, and why the name got louder than the evidence. Voice is [brand-voice.md](brand-voice.md). Claim class is literature, not `cosmetic_appearance`. The serum ask is one line at the end. This page does not change the science pillar in [storefront-copy.md](storefront-copy.md). That pillar still holds the evidence rooms. This one holds the timeline.

No injection steps, no mixing rates, no personal protocol. Words such as collagen, wound, and injectable appear only as labels on what a paper or a market actually was.

## Why this page exists

Readers arrive already sure the ingredient is famous. The fame has three sources that get flattened into one sentence: a 1973 liver-cell paper, a 1990s cosmetic launch, and a 2018 price drop. This draft keeps those as separate rooms, then says which one actually made the bottle common.

## Page

**Eyebrow** — A timeline. Papers stay papers.

**Headline** — How GHK-Cu became a household serum.

**Deck** — Fifty years from a blue fraction in human plasma to a bottle you can order on a Tuesday. The rise is real. Most of it is commercial, not a new human trial.

### The molecule, in one breath

GHK-Cu is glycyl-L-histidyl-L-lysine bound to copper(II). Three amino acids. On a cosmetic label the INCI name is Copper Tripeptide-1. The complex is blue. That color is why later creams photographed well and why a serum looks like it is “doing something” before you have read a word.

### 1973: a plasma fraction, measured on liver cells

Loren Pickart, then at UCSF, and Marvin Thaler reported a small factor in human serum. Their paper in *Nature New Biology* (1973) said the tripeptide prolonged survival of normal liver cells in culture and stimulated growth of neoplastic liver cells. The setting is cultured liver cells. It is not a face, and it is not a skin claim.

The story that travels — young plasma changing the behavior of older tissue — comes from how that work was later told. The 1973 paper itself is a cell result about a tripeptide in serum.

### 1980: the copper is the point

While isolating the peptide, the group kept finding copper with it. Pickart, Freedman, and colleagues, *Nature* (1980), reported that GHK readily forms a complex with copper(II) and increases copper uptake into cultured hepatoma cells. They noted a resemblance to the copper-binding site on albumin.

That paper answers a mechanism question in cells: the peptide holds copper and cells take the metal up. It does not measure wrinkles, firmness, or a cosmetic formula.

### 1988: the collagen sentence everyone repeats

Maquart and colleagues, *FEBS Letters* (1988), reported more collagen synthesis in cultured fibroblasts treated with the copper complex. Laboratory class. Useful if you are asking what fibroblasts did in a dish. It is not a result on intact skin, and it is not what a serum in this shop claims.

Through the 1990s, further papers, including work from the same groups, measured the complex in animal wound models. Animal class. Those results do not transfer to people on their own, and they do not transfer to a cosmetic applied to unbroken skin.

### Late 1980s–2001: the clinic, then the drugstore

The name left the journal when a company carried it.

ProCyte Corporation, in Redmond, Washington, worked on GHK copper-peptide technology from the mid-1980s, first around acute wound-care products. Pickart helped develop that technology and left the company in 1991. He later sold his own formulas through Skin Biology. A careful reader should not treat every “copper peptide” cream from that era as GHK-Cu at a stated percent. Some classic copper products use a hydrolyzed-protein copper complex. Identity is the INCI and the assay, not the color blue.

Trade reporting in *Women’s Wear Daily* places the cosmetic wave like this:

- Osmotics sold Blue Copper Firming Elasticity Repair from 1998, under a ProCyte license.
- ProCyte introduced Neova in May 2000, sold through physician offices.
- Neutrogena licensed the technology and prepared Visibly Firm night and eye creams for mass retail, described in that report as arriving in stores in May.

That is the first popularity. A physician line, a prestige jar, then a Neutrogena launch large enough that the trade press called copper peptide a candidate for the next retinol. The claim language on those launches was of its time: rebuild, firm, restore. We do not import it. What we take from the episode is distribution. “Copper peptide” became a phrase a shopper had already heard.

Patents and licenses made the ingredient scarce and expensive. When those exclusive positions aged, more labels could print Copper Tripeptide-1. Fame did not spike on the day a patent ended. It waited for a price people would actually try.

### The long middle

Between the drugstore creams and the late 2010s, GHK-Cu was a known cosmetic ingredient and a standing topic in ingredient write-ups. It was not the default serum in a routine. Cost was the practical reason. Deciem’s own 2018 note said GHK was expensive, that NIOD’s Copper Amino Isolate Serum existed because of that cost, and that a cheaper 1% copper-peptide serum would not fit the retailers who carried NIOD.

NIOD’s CAIS had already made the ingredient a cult object: a watery blue liquid, a two-part mix on early versions, a price that marked it as a specialist step. Cult is not the same as common.

### 2018: the price became the popularity

On 5 April 2018, Deciem told its audience that The Ordinary “Buffet” + Copper Peptides 1% was coming the following Wednesday, and that it would be affordable. A January 2018 note from the same founder had already promised that serum for spring, as the low-price home for a GHK-Cu product that NIOD’s retailers were a poor place to sell.

That launch is the second popularity, and it is the one that stuck. A famous cheap brand put the words “copper peptides” on a bottle a routine spreadsheet could absorb. Forums and later short video treated the ingredient as a step you were late to, the way vitamin C and retinol had been.

Read the name with the label. “1%” on that product is the brand’s name for a peptide blend that includes Copper Tripeptide-1. It is not, by itself, proof that the bottle is 1% GHK-Cu. Our serum prints the Copper Tripeptide-1 percent on its own label. A blend name and a stated percent are different facts.

The Ordinary did not publish a new human trial to earn that moment. The molecule was the same one from 1973, 1980, and 1988. What changed was access.

### After that: lore outruns the bottle

Once the serum was common, the story got longer than the cosmetic.

Social video likes a fifty-year origin, a blue liquid, and a single collagen sentence. The sentence is still Maquart 1988, still cells. Reviews that summarize gene-expression papers are still reviews. They are only as strong as the papers under them, and they are not a test of a finished serum.

A separate market grew up beside the cosmetic one: compounded preparations and powders sold with a research wink. That market is not this catalog. Cosmetic Copper Tripeptide-1 on intact skin, a formulator powder with a lot and a COA, and any injectable or oral use are three objects. We sell the first. We sell the second only as an ingredient, with the formulator notice. We do not explain how to make the third.

### What actually made it popular

Four things, in order, none of them a new blinded trial on our bottle:

1. A short name and a visible blue complex, tied to a real 1973–1980 chemistry story.
2. A 1998–2001 path from a licensed prestige cream to physician offices to mass retail, which taught shoppers the phrase.
3. A 2018 price drop under a brand people already trusted for cheap serums.
4. Social retelling that compresses cell papers, animal wound papers, and cosmetic use into one benefit.

The fourth is why this page is long. Popularity is not evidence class. A dish, a mouse, a person, and a moisturizer are four sentences.

### What we claim at the end of the story

Studies of the molecule are linked from the science page, labeled with their setting. We have not run a finished-product test on our serum. When we do, it gets its own line there.

The serum is a cosmetic. It is for the look of fine lines and the feel of moisturized skin. The percent is printed. The lot is on the glass.
